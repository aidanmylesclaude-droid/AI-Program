## Key terms
- **LLM (large language model):** a program trained on a huge amount of text to predict what text comes next. Claude is one.
- **Token:** the chunk of text a model reads and writes. Often a whole short word, sometimes part of a long word or a punctuation mark. Usage, cost and limits are counted in tokens.
- **Training:** the process, done *before* you ever use the model, that adjusts it so its predictions get better.
- **Parameters (weights):** the billions of numbers inside a model that training adjusts. They're what the model "knows".
- **Generation:** producing a reply by predicting one token, adding it, and repeating.
- **Sampling:** picking the next token with some randomness, so likely tokens win most of the time but not always.
- **Hallucination:** a fluent, confident statement that is false. It happens because the model produces *plausible* text, not checked facts.
- **Knowledge cutoff:** the date the training text stops. Anything newer, the model only knows if a tool (search, docs, your files) shows it.

## The problem this solves
Every later topic (agents, tools, memory, MCP) is a way of working around what an LLM is and isn't. If you know it's a next-token predictor that forgets between sessions and can be confidently wrong, the reasons for `CLAUDE.md`, `PROGRESS.md`, "check the official docs" and permission prompts all make sense.

## Explanation
**1. It predicts the next token.** Give a model "the market opened" and it estimates which token is likely to come next. Add it, predict again, repeat. A whole reply, even a long, careful one, is produced that way.

**2. Training sets the odds.** Training showed the model enormous amounts of text and nudged its parameters until its predictions improved. Then further training shaped it into a helpful assistant that follows instructions. All of this happened *before* our conversation.

**3. It doesn't learn from our chat.** My parameters don't change while we talk. Within one session I can see the conversation (its *context*). Next session, I start blank. Everything I "remember" about you comes from files I read at the start: `~\.claude\CLAUDE.md`, `AI-Program\CLAUDE.md`, `PROGRESS.md`. That's why we built the program on files.

**4. Sampling explains varied answers.** With some randomness in each pick, the same question can produce different wording or even different answers.

**5. Plausible is not the same as true.** The model produces text that *fits*. Usually that matches reality, but not always: that's hallucination. It's why the program rule says to check official docs for anything current, and why tools that look things up matter.

**6. What we can't see.** Parameters are billions of numbers, not readable code. Researchers (including Anthropic's interpretability team) can trace some internal patterns, but no one can read off a full, reliable account of *why* a model produced a given answer. When I explain my reasoning, that explanation is itself generated text. It's often useful, but it isn't a direct readout of what happened inside.

**Toy vs real.** The exercise script counts which word follows which, using only the *one* previous word and 30 words of training text. A real LLM looks at everything in its context, uses tokens instead of whole words, and has billions of parameters trained on vastly more text. It's the same basic idea at a scale that makes it seem like understanding. Whether and how much it *is* understanding is genuinely debated.

## Example from your projects
**Trading assistant:** an LLM predicts *text*, not *prices*. Asked "will SPY go up?", it will produce a fluent, plausible answer, which is exactly why the program's rule says to state uncertainty and never imply guaranteed profits. Real market data must come from a tool (an API), not from the model's memory.
