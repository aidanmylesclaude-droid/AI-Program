# 1.2.7 Limits: hallucination, outdated knowledge, overconfidence, and how to catch them
**Stage / Module:** 1 / 1.2 · **Status:** done · **Date taught:** 2026-11-17
**Builds on:** 1.2.1 (plausible ≠ true, knowledge cutoff), 1.2.3 (OBSERVE and VERIFY), 1.2.6 (ask for evidence). Last lesson of Module 1.2.

## What you'll learn
- The four ways an LLM is most often wrong
- Real times Claude was wrong in *this* program, and what caught it
- A short checklist for catching mistakes before they cost you

## Key terms
- **Hallucination** (from 1.2.1): a fluent, confident statement that's false.
- **Knowledge cutoff:** the date the training text stops. Claude's is around mid-2026. Anything newer, or anything that changed since, it can only know through a tool or a file.
- **Overconfidence:** sounding just as sure when wrong as when right. An LLM's *tone* doesn't tell you how accurate it is.
- **Sycophancy:** an AI's tendency to agree with you, or to go along with how a question is framed ("isn't it true that…?"), even when you're wrong.
- **Ground truth:** what's actually the case, as shown by the computer, the official source, or real data. It's what you check claims against.

## The problem this solves
Everything you've built with AI depends on catching its mistakes. On your projects a wrong claim can cost real things: a misread price or indicator (trading), a post that breaks a platform rule (content), or a wrong supplier cost (e-commerce). You can't stop an LLM being wrong sometimes. You *can* make sure the mistakes get caught.

## Explanation
**The four common failures**
| Failure | What it looks like | Riskiest when |
|---|---|---|
| **Hallucination** | Invented details: numbers, versions, names, links, command options that don't exist | Specific facts you can't immediately check |
| **Outdated knowledge** | True once, not now: old prices, old tool features, old platform rules | Anything that changes: software, policies, markets |
| **Overconfidence** | The same calm tone whether right or wrong | Always. Tone is not evidence |
| **Sycophancy** | Agreeing with your mistaken idea; "yes, great plan!" | When you ask leading questions or seem attached to an idea |

**Claude was wrong in this program, and here's what caught it**
| Claim | Reality | Caught by |
|---|---|---|
| `CLAUDE.md`: "Python 3.13 is installed" (marked *verified*) | `py --version` → 3.15.2; plain `python` didn't even work | Running it on the PC (1.1.5) |
| 1.1.5 draft: JSON price would show as `512.3` | It showed `512.30` | Testing the exercise before teaching it |
| 1.1.3: `01-University` contains lessons and three files | It also had `exercises` | **Your** `ls` output |
Notice that even a note marked "verified" went out of date. Checks expire.

**How to catch mistakes: the checklist**
1. **Run it.** If the claim is about your PC or files, check it with a command (ground truth beats memory).
2. **Check the official source.** For tool features, prices, platform rules: the vendor's own docs, with a date.
3. **Ask for evidence** (1.2.6): the command and output, or the link and the exact quote.
4. **Ask how sure, and what would prove it wrong.** "How confident are you, and how could I check?" is a good question to ask an AI.
5. **Don't lead the witness.** Ask "is X true?" rather than "X is true, right?", which avoids triggering sycophancy.
6. **Scale checking to the stakes.** A word count needs a glance. A trade idea or a platform-policy question needs a source.

## Example from your projects
- **Trading:** "SPY's RSI is 72, so it's overbought." Check: was RSI computed from *today's* data by a tool, or recalled? What period? A number with no source is a guess.
- **Content:** "TikTok allows 3 posts a day per account." Platform rules change. Check TikTok's own current policy page, and note the date.
- **E-commerce:** "This supplier's unit cost is $2.40." Check: where's the quote, and from when?

## Hands-on exercise: catch Claude
Claude makes four claims about your PC. **At least one is deliberately false.** For each: predict true or false, then check it with the command.
| # | Claim | Check it with |
|---|---|---|
| A | This PC's PowerShell is version 5.1 | `$PSVersionTable.PSVersion` |
| B | Your global `CLAUDE.md` is 20 lines long | `(Get-Content ~\.claude\CLAUDE.md).Count` |
| C | There are 15 lesson files in `01-University\lessons` | `(Get-ChildItem ~\AI-Program\01-University\lessons).Count` |
| D | `cat` is an alias for `Get-ChildItem` | `Get-Command cat` |
(`.Count` means "how many": lines in a file, or items in a folder.)
