## Research
- **Myth:** Goldfish only remember things for about three seconds, so every lap of the bowl feels new to them.
- **Type:** Factoid
- **Why people believe it:** It doesn't come from any study. Live Science calls it a complete myth that, despite its lack of scientific backing, has spread widely and remained largely undisputed by the public for decades. The exact number changes depending on who's telling it: "In some places, it's 2 seconds, and in others, it's 10 — but it's always short." NPR's Short Wave said the phrase probably weren't born out of peer-reviewed science. One theory for why it stuck is that guilt from pet owners may be another factor in understating goldfish memory. ABC News also pointed out that Disney's Finding Nemo - in which one of the main characters is a dory who suffers from short-term memory loss - have done little to help dispel the myth.
- **The truth:** Goldfish learn and remember well. In the Plymouth lever study, the fish were first trained to press a lever for food. Then the feeding time was gradually restricted to one hour per day, and the fish concentrated more of their lever pressing around the period when food was available, showing that they could learn and anticipate a regular daily feeding schedule. NPR's expert source agreed. Goldfish memory lasts weeks, at least. Fish cognition expert Culum Brown says goldfish memories span weeks, months and even years, Brown said. And the science to back this up has been around for more than 60 years. Lever work with goldfish goes back decades. A paper in *Science* reported that goldfish were taught to press a lever for food reinforcement and were placed on a 1-minute fixed-interval schedule. They developed the characteristic temporal discrimination (scalloping) seen in rats and pigeons.
- **Bonus fact (it gets weirder):** At Keio University in Japan, goldfish were divided into two groups and trained to bite a red bead when played short sections of either Bach's Toccata and Fugue in D Minor or Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring. The peer-reviewed paper reports that goldfish successfully discriminated between two musical stimuli (Experiment 1). Goldfish did not show consistent preferences for musical stimuli (Experiment 2A). NBC News says the researchers concluded the fish didn't seem to prefer one to the other, and that the study will be published in the October edition of the journal Behavioural Processes. (That was 2013, per the DOI 10.1016/j.beproc.2013.06.009.)
- **Nuance / partly true:** There's no single "real" number to swap in. It is hard to pinpoint an exact time span for goldfish memory, given that different memories are more notable than others. The script says "weeks, even months" and avoids any exact figure. The Plymouth study itself tested daily timing, not a fixed retention period. The study was not itself a three-month memory test, but other work has found retention over that kind of period. The script never claims Plymouth proved "three months."
- **Sources:**

  - https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2190&context=bts is an academic article that quotes the Gee study directly. It supports "gradually restricted to one hour per day."
  - https://www.npr.org/2020/01/08/794625042/myth-busting-blind-as-a-bat-and-memory-of-a-goldfish (NPR Short Wave): a fish-memory researcher says goldfish memory lasts weeks, and goldfish were trained to push a lever at a certain time of day for food.
  - https://www.livescience.com/goldfish-memory.html (Live Science, quoting Culum Brown of Macquarie University) supports: no scientific basis, the "2 or 10 seconds" variation, weeks to months, 60+ years of science, and pet-owner guilt as a possible factor.
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14330528/ (PubMed, *Science* 149:561) shows goldfish trained on lever-timing tasks in the 1960s. Background only, not in the script.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376635713001228 is the primary Bach/Stravinsky study (Behavioural Processes, 2013). It supports discrimination between the two pieces and no consistent preference.
  - https://www.nbcnews.com/sciencemain/dont-try-fool-goldfish-they-know-their-bach-stravinsky-4b11187601 (NBC News/LiveScience) independently reports the Keio study and the lack of a preference.

  - https://www.pri.org/stories/2013-09-02/study-goldfish-listen-music-prefer-bach backs up the red bead detail and reports "closer to three months" for memory. Its "prefer Bach" claim conflicts with the primary study and was not used.
