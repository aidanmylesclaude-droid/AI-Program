## Research
- **Myth:** The color red makes bulls angry, and that is why they charge a matador's red cape.
- **Type:** Misconception.
- **Why people believe it:** In the final stage of a bullfight, the matador enters with a small red cape called a muleta. The bull charges it, so people assumed the color caused the charge. The idiom "red rag to a bull" and cartoons then spread the idea. The usual explanation of the idiom connects it with bullfighting.
- **The truth:** The color is not the trigger. The capote used in the first act is magenta on the front and yellow or blue inside, and bulls charge either color as readily as they charge the red muleta. It is motion that provokes the charge. The bull is likely irritated by the cape's movement as the matador whips it around. The eyes explain part of it. Cows, goats, sheep, horses and pigs appear to lack red (L) cones, so they have the photopigment basis for dichromatic color vision. Cattle have two cone types, not our three. Fighting bulls are also a specially selected breed. They have traditionally been selected for aggressiveness, ferocity and mobility. The breed is characterized by genetic selection for a behavior called "bravery".
- **Nuance / partly true:**
  - Bulls are not "blind" to color. They see color with two cone types and have trouble with red-green differences. Some outlets say "colorblind to red" or "red looks gray", which is oversimplified. The script avoids those phrases. Cattle can distinguish red from grey (Riol et al., 1989) and red from green (Phillips & Lomas, 2001).
  - One lab study found calves moved more under long-wavelength (red) light and responded to a handler. They had stronger movement in the long than the short or medium wavelength light in the novel arena test and in response to the handler. That is not rage, and it was not tested on charging bulls. It is a reason not to claim that red has literally zero effect on cattle behavior.
  - The "red hides blood" explanation is a common, plausible reason for the tradition. Britannica says red minimizes the appearance of blood and other stains and makes a more colorful spectacle. Snopes found no definitive evidence that muletas are red specifically to mask blood. The script says red "hides stains", which is an effect, and does not claim it is the proven reason.
- **Bonus fact (the idiom):**
  - In the 17th century, "red rag" was slang for the tongue. Worldwidewords dates that meaning to about 1600.
  - A red rag was a piece of red cloth used to provoke an animal, especially a bull, and in early use a fowl. Bulls come a long way down the list of red-rag-sensitive beings in early citations.
  - Pheasants appear in an early quoted example. The script says "birds, like pheasants" because I found no precise "first animal" date I could source twice.
- **Sources:**

I did not find a peer-reviewed controlled test of movement vs color in charging bulls. The "motion" claim therefore rests on Britannica, Live Science and a TV experiment reported by Snopes, and the script hedges with "most likely".
